Understanding audio-pitch-correction in MPV

When changing playback speed in the mpv media player, the audio-pitch-correction setting controls whether the audio frequency shifts alongside the tempo or stays at its original tonal height. This article explains the technical purpose of this feature, how it prevents audio distortion during speed changes, and how to manage the setting depending on your listening preferences.

The Purpose of Audio Pitch Correction

By default, speeding up or slowing down an audio track produces an analog-style frequency shift. Increasing playback speed raises the frequency, causing voices to sound squeaky and high-pitched (commonly known as the "chipmunk effect"). Conversely, lowering playback speed drops the frequency, producing an unnaturally deep, sluggish voice.

The audio-pitch-correction option in mpv prevents these shifts. When enabled, it allows users to change the playback speed—such as speeding up a video to 1.5x or 2.0x—while preserving the original pitch of voices, instruments, and background sounds.

How It Works

Under the hood, mpv uses a time-stretching audio filter (such as scaletempo or scaletempo2) to decouple the playback tempo from the audio pitch.

Instead of simply resampling the audio stream faster or slower, the filter slices the audio into small overlapping segments (grains) and shifts their alignment in time. This technique compresses or expands the duration of the audio waveform without changing the fundamental frequencies of the content.

When to Keep It Enabled

The option is enabled by default (audio-pitch-correction=yes). You should keep it active when:

  • Watching lectures, tutorials, or podcasts at accelerated speeds to save time.
  • Slowing down fast dialogue in a foreign language to improve comprehension.
  • Reviewing video footage where maintaining natural vocal characteristics is essential.

When to Disable It

Setting audio-pitch-correction=no turns off the time-stretching filter. You might want to disable it if:

  • Minimizing Artifacts: Time-stretching algorithms can introduce subtle acoustic artifacts, such as phase smearing, robotic timbre, or stuttering, especially on complex musical tracks.
  • Creative or Vinyl Effects: You intentionally want the classic tape-speed or turntable effect where pitch scales proportionally with speed.
  • Performance on Low-Power Devices: Bypassing the filter reduces processing overhead slightly, though modern hardware rarely struggles with this filter.

How to Configure It

You can control this behavior directly via the command line, within your configuration file, or dynamically during playback.

To disable it by default in your mpv.conf file, add:

audio-pitch-correction=no

To run a specific file without pitch correction via the command line:

mpv --audio-pitch-correction=no video.mp4

You can also bind a key in input.conf to toggle the setting on the fly:

Alt+p cycle audio-pitch-correction
