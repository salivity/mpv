mpv Interpolation vs TV Motion Smoothing

While both technologies aim to eliminate motion judder on modern digital displays, they rely on fundamentally different technical approaches. Traditional television motion smoothing uses motion-compensated frame interpolation (MEMC) to predict object trajectories and generate entirely new intermediate frames, which often introduces noticeable artifacts and creates the unnatural "soap opera effect." In contrast, the open-source media player mpv uses temporal resampling to align existing video frames with your display's native refresh rate, eliminating timing mismatch judder while preserving the video's original cinematic motion cadence.

How Traditional TV Motion Smoothing (MEMC) Works

Most televisions equipped with motion smoothing use Motion Estimation, Motion Compensation (MEMC) hardware. This process involves analyzing two consecutive frames, calculating the movement vectors of objects across the screen, and rendering synthetic in-between frames.

If a movie shot at 24 frames per second (fps) is displayed on a 120Hz panel, an MEMC algorithm manufactures multiple artificial frames to transition an object smoothly from Point A to Point B. While this results in hyper-fluid motion, it comes with significant drawbacks:

  • The Soap Opera Effect: High-motion fluidity strips away the visual characteristics of 24fps cinema, making cinematic productions look like live television or raw digital video.
  • Interpolation Artifacts: Complex motion—such as rain, fast-moving particles, overlapping objects, or transparent elements—frequently confuses motion vectors, generating visible distortions, haloing, and tearing around edges.
  • Input Latency: Calculating vector fields requires deep buffering and heavy processing, introducing noticeable input lag that makes it impractical for interactive media.

How mpv Handles Video Interpolation

The mpv media player approaches the problem as a display synchronization and temporal resampling issue rather than a frame-generation task. Built around the --interpolation and --video-sync=display-resample flags, mpv addresses the judder caused by uneven frame distribution, such as the standard 3:2 pulldown required to display 24fps content on a standard 60Hz monitor.

Instead of guessing where pixels are moving and generating new frames, mpv blends overlapping source frames across the timeline based on the precise refresh intervals of the display. It treats the video track as a continuous signal over time and applies a linear or non-linear filter (configured via the --tscale option, such as oversample, mitchell, or bicubic) to sample the original frames onto the display refresh cycles.

Key characteristics of mpv's method include:

  • Preservation of Cadence: Because it does not extrapolate intermediate positions, the original 24fps motion characteristics remain intact. The footage does not look like high-frame-rate video.
  • No Vector Glitches: Without motion estimation, mpv cannot produce the tearing, ghosting, or halo artifacts typical of TV processors.
  • Controlled Blurring Instead of Fake Sharpness: If an original frame does not align perfectly with a screen refresh cycle, mpv smoothly blends the adjacent frames based on the chosen temporal filter. This can introduce slight motion softness in specific configurations, but it avoids jarring visual errors.

The Fundamental Difference

The distinction comes down to frame synthesis versus temporal distribution.

Traditional televisions alter the temporal resolution of the content itself by inventing frames that never existed in the source file. The mpv interpolation engine simply ensures that the frames that do exist are presented at mathematically uniform time intervals across the monitor’s refresh cycles. Televisions prioritize hyper-smoothness at the expense of creative intent and image accuracy, whereas mpv prioritizes timing accuracy and the preservation of the source material.
