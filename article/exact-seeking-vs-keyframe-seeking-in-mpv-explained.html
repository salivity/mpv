Exact Seeking vs Keyframe Seeking in MPV Explained

This article breaks down the technical and practical differences between exact seeking and keyframe seeking in the mpv media player. It explores how modern video compression affects media navigation, compares the speed and precision of both methods, explains mpv's default behaviors, and helps you determine which mode best fits your viewing or editing workflow.

Understanding Modern Video Compression

To understand seeking modes, you must understand how modern video formats (such as H.264, H.265, and VP9) store data. Videos are not merely sequences of complete pictures; they consist of Groups of Pictures (GOP) featuring different frame types:

  • Intra-frames (I-frames / Keyframes): Complete, self-contained images that can be rendered independently.
  • Predicted frames (P-frames and B-frames): Incomplete frames that only store the changes between neighboring frames to save space.

Because of this structure, a player cannot instantly display a random P-frame or B-frame in the middle of a video without first decoding the keyframe that precedes it.

Keyframe Seeking (Fast Seeking)

Keyframe seeking instructs mpv to jump directly to the nearest keyframe relative to your target timestamp.

  • How it works: When you jump forward or backward, mpv finds the closest complete keyframe and starts playback immediately from that point.
  • Speed: Instantaneous. The video decoder does not need to process intermediate frames, resulting in zero playback stutter or lag.
  • Precision: Low. Because keyframes are often placed several seconds apart (typically every 1 to 10 seconds depending on encoding settings), playback will not start at your exact requested second. It will snap to whenever the encoder placed the nearest I-frame.
  • Resource Usage: Minimal CPU and GPU overhead.

Exact Seeking

Exact seeking forces mpv to navigate to the precise timestamp or frame requested, regardless of where the keyframes are located.

  • How it works: When an exact seek is requested, mpv jumps backward to the nearest preceding keyframe, silently decodes all subsequent P-frames and B-frames in the background, and displays the exact target frame requested.
  • Speed: Slower. There may be a perceptible delay or brief freeze while the player decodes the required chain of frames before resuming visible playback. This delay is more pronounced on weak hardware or with high-bitrate 4K/8K media.
  • Precision: Absolute. You land on the precise second or frame you requested.
  • Resource Usage: Higher CPU/GPU decoding demand during navigation.

MPV Keybindings and Configuration

By default, mpv balances both behaviors depending on the key binding:

  • Standard Arrow Keys (LEFT/RIGHT, UP/DOWN): Typically mapped to keyframe seeking for fast, responsive scrubbing through media.
  • Shift + Arrow Keys: Often mapped by default to exact seeking, allowing users to make precise, small-step adjustments when needed.

You can explicitly control this behavior in your input.conf file using mpv's command syntax:

# Exact seek: jumps precisely 5 seconds forward
RIGHT seek 5 exact

# Keyframe seek: jumps roughly 5 seconds forward to the nearest I-frame
UP seek 5 keyframes

When to Use Each Mode

  • Choose Keyframe Seeking for casual video playback, skipping intro sequences, jumping through long movies, or browsing media on low-powered devices.
  • Choose Exact Seeking for video editing, frame-by-frame analysis, audio/subtitle synchronization, educational study, or transcribing dialog where exact timing is critical.
